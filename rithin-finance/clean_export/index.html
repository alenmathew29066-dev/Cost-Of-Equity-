Understanding Why Methods Produce Different Results
Methods may produce different results because each method uses different assumptions and financial information. For instance, the Dividend Price Method assumes zero future growth, while the Gordon model prices in continuous dividend expansion. The Earnings Price Method reflects total corporate earning power (assuming retention adds equal value), while Realised Yield reflects historical past performance rather than future expectations.

Finance Principle: No single method is universally declared the "best" method. The appropriate method depends on the firm’s dividend policy, growth stage, and market predictability.

METHOD 1: ZERO GROWTH

Dividend Price Method

The Dividend Price Method assumes that the market price of an equity share depends strictly on the current cash dividend paid to shareholders, and that this dividend will remain constant forever.

Ke = (D / P) × 100
  • Ke = Cost of Equity capital (percentage).
  • D = Dividend per Share (DPS).
  • P = Current Market Price per Share (MPS).
  • When used: Ideal for mature, zero-growth companies that pay out 100% of their earnings as steady dividends.
  • Limitation: Unrealistic for growing modern firms that retain earnings or increase dividends annually.
Solved Example: A firm pays a dividend of ₹8 per share and its market price is ₹100.
Ke = (8 / 100) × 100 = 8.00%
METHOD 2: GORDON MODEL

Dividend Yield + Growth Method

Recognizes that equity investors expect dividends to increase over time as company profits expand. The cost of equity equals the current dividend yield plus the anticipated dividend growth rate ($g$).

Ke = (D1 / P0) + g
  • D1 = Expected dividend next year = D0 × (1 + g).
  • P0 = Current market price per share.
  • g = Constant annual dividend growth rate.
  • When used: Widely used in corporate finance and MBA exams for established firms with steady growth.
  • Limitation: Requires that growth rate ($g$) is less than Cost of Equity ($Ke$), and growth must remain constant.
Solved Example: Current div = ₹8, growth = 5%, price = ₹100.
D1 = 8 × 1.05 = ₹8.40
Ke = (8.40 / 100) + 0.05 = 0.084 + 0.05 = 13.40%
METHOD 3: EARNINGS YIELD

Earnings Price Method

The Earnings Price Method argues that equity shareholders own the entire earnings of the company, whether paid out as dividends or retained. Therefore, Cost of Equity is the company’s earnings yield.

Ke = (E / P) × 100
  • E = Earnings per Share (EPS = Net Profit after Tax & Pref. Div / No. of Equity Shares).
  • P = Market Price per Share.
  • When used: Highly useful when companies retain a high proportion of profits for reinvestment.
  • Limitation: Assumes retained earnings will generate a rate of return exactly equal to current cost of equity.
Solved Example: EPS = ₹12, Market Price = ₹120.
Ke = (12 / 120) × 100 = 10.00% (P/E ratio = 10x)
METHOD 4: HISTORICAL RETURN

Realised Yield Method

Instead of forecasting future dividends or earnings, this method measures the actual return realised by an investor in past years through cash dividends and share price appreciation.

Realised Yield = [(D + (P1 - P0)) / P0] × 100
  • P0 = Beginning market price.
  • P1 = Ending market price.
  • D = Dividend received during the holding period.
  • (P1 - P0) = Capital Gain (or loss if negative).
  • When used: When future projections are uncertain and past performance provides a realistic proxy.
Solved Example: Bought at ₹100, sold at ₹110, dividend = ₹5.
Yield = [5 + (110 - 100)] / 100 × 100 = 15.00%

Why Cost of Equity Matters

In Corporate Financial Management, the Cost of Equity Capital (Ke) represents the minimum rate of return a firm must generate on equity-financed investments to maintain the market value of its common stock. Unlike debt capital which carries an explicit contractual interest rate, equity capital does not have a legally binding payment obligation. Estimating its cost requires financial models that translate investor expectations, dividend policies, and market returns into an accurate percentage hurdle rate.
